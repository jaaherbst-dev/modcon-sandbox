The Still Grounded Dispatch

The system is working exactly as intended. That's the problem.

Sunday 2 August 2026

Every institution in this edition is performing precisely as designed. Central banks hike while governments quietly expire the rebates that made the numbers look good. Defence alliances spend trillions and call it peace. Tech companies post record revenue, fire a hundred thousand workers, and call it progress. The pattern is not dysfunction. The pattern is the product.

Australian Affairs Desk

Inflation falls to 4%, which is still double the target, but we're celebrating

Australia's annual CPI inflation eased to 4.0% in May, down from the 4.6% spike in March. The Treasurer called the numbers 'encouraging.' The mechanism behind the spike was never mysterious — the Commonwealth and state government electricity rebates expired, and household power bills surged over 21% in a single year. Strip out the rebate effect and electricity only rose about 3.9%. So the government temporarily suppressed the number, took credit for low inflation during an election cycle, then let the rebate lapse and called the rebound a global problem. The RBA hiked to 4.35% in May. Your mortgage and your power bill both got more expensive so the chart could look better twelve months ago.

Australian Affairs Desk

Rent is up, power is up, wages are sort of up, everything is fine

Perth's median house rent hit a record $740 a week in the March quarter, up 5.7%. Housing inflation nationally is running at 6.5%. The 2026–27 Budget offered a tax rate cut from 16% to 15% on incomes between $18,201 and $45,000, which delivers up to $268 a year — roughly one week of the rent increase in Perth. The Fair Work Commission is reviewing award wages while the government recommends 'an economically sustainable real wage increase,' which is code for not enough to actually close the gap. The cost of living crisis is not a crisis to the institutions managing it. It is the system performing exactly as designed.

Tech and Power Desk

AI eats 170,000 jobs, and the companies posting record revenue would like you to know it's not personal

Across 322 layoff events tracked in 2026 so far, 54% explicitly cite AI, automation, or machine learning as a factor — affecting roughly 170,000 workers. Oracle's 30,000-role reduction is the single largest. Microsoft cut 4,800 positions. Block went from 10,000 employees to fewer than 6,000, with CEO Jack Dorsey offering the rare candour of saying it wasn't about financial difficulty — just that AI could now do the work. Companies are posting record revenues and eliminating the people who generated them, and the share of layoffs blamed on AI leapt from 7% in January to 40% by May — faster, as one analysis noted, than the technology itself advanced. AI is functioning partly as a rationale investors reward.

Tech and Power Desk

Europe's AI rules arrive on schedule — minus the parts that mattered

Today was supposed to be the day the EU AI Act grew teeth. Instead, the Omnibus deferral pushed high-risk AI rules — the ones covering recruitment tools, credit scoring, law enforcement, and border control — to December 2027 at the earliest, with product-embedded AI getting until August 2028. What did arrive on schedule? Transparency obligations: chatbot disclosure and deepfake labelling. The uncomfortable stuff — the rules that would have forced real compliance from the systems that actually shape people's lives — got a quiet 16-month extension because member states hadn't finished appointing regulators and the standards weren't ready. The regulatory sandbox deadline also slid to 2027. Europe's first-mover advantage in AI governance now looks more like a first-mover announcement.

Surveillance capitalism left the internet and moved into oil, luxury goods, and your car

A Harvard Carr-Ryan Center study found that surveillance capitalism is no longer confined to Big Tech. Firms across oil and gas, luxury, and mass consumer goods are now building internal capabilities to collect, analyse, and monetise personal data. Your car, meanwhile, increasingly resembles a surveillance device that occasionally permits transportation. The infrastructure of behavioural prediction has quietly embedded itself in industries that never used to need your data — but discovered they wanted the revenue.

Geopolitical Desk

Drone hits Egyptian port, because one closed shipping lane was not enough

A drone struck a US-owned LNG vessel at Damietta, near the Suez Canal, marking the first attack on Egyptian soil in the five-month Iran war. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since February, the Houthis have declared a naval blockade on Saudi Arabia via the Red Sea, and now the last open workaround is catching fire. The Pentagon, having promised a few weeks of bombing in February, is now managing a three-front maritime crisis with no off-ramp in sight.

NATO agrees to spend 5 percent of GDP on defence and calls it keeping the peace

At the Ankara Summit, NATO allies noted they have increased core defence investments by more than USD 139 billion since The Hague, with a target of 5 percent of GDP by 2035. Meanwhile, Handelsblatt reports NATO is preparing to strengthen nuclear deterrence in Europe, with Poland, Lithuania and Finland being considered for new US warhead deployments. The word 'peace' appears nowhere in the communiqué, but 'industrial resilience' and 'lethality' do, which tells you everything about the product being sold.

Washington sanctions everyone, Beijing objects on principle, and SWIFT keeps the lights on

Proposed US legislation would impose tariffs of up to 100 percent on leading buyers of Russian oil and gas, including China. Beijing responded by opposing unilateral sanctions that lack UN Security Council authorisation, calling them economic coercion while simultaneously pushing its own anti-coercion agenda at the Security Council. The sanctions regime has become the preferred American weapon: no boots on the ground, just SWIFT access as leverage and the quiet understanding that the dollar is still the room everyone has to walk through.

Australian Affairs Desk

The government would like to count you and also ask about your love life

The 2026 Census arrives on August 11, and the ABS has added new questions on sex recorded at birth, gender identity, and sexual orientation. You can now also list four ancestries instead of two, which is generous. The stated purpose is better data for better policy. The unstated reality is that governments have had decades of census data and still can't build enough houses or fund enough hospitals. But sure, maybe the missing variable was how many non-binary Australians live in Wollongong. The scammers are already circling — the ABS is warning about fake census communications. Because naturally, a form asking for your most personal details is also the one most easily impersonated.

Still Grounded

The institutions are not broken. They are doing exactly what they were built to do — measuring, spending, cutting, sanctioning, surveilling — and none of it was built for you. The data gets better. The housing doesn't. The weapons get funded. The peace doesn't. Somewhere in the gap between the chart and the lived experience, the whole arrangement quietly reproduces itself.
