The Still Grounded Dispatch

Institutions build the flood, then sell you the sandbags

Sunday 9 August 2026

This week's pattern is not complicated. Institutions created the conditions, took credit when things looked manageable, and are now rebranding the consequences as someone else's problem. Supermarkets ship jobs offshore while selling groceries to the people they just stopped employing. Governments let rebates expire and call rising bills a market outcome. Safety companies file IPO paperwork. The mechanisms are different. The logic is identical.

Australian Affairs Desk

Coles finds cheaper humans elsewhere

Supermarket giant Coles confirmed it is shipping jobs offshore through a new arrangement with Accenture, because a company that made its fortune selling groceries to Australians apparently cannot justify employing them. CEO Leah Weckert communicated the news to staff via internal email, which is the corporate equivalent of breaking up by text. The cost savings will presumably not be passed on to customers at checkout.

Australian Affairs Desk

The rebate era is over. The bill era is back.

Household electricity bills are now 25.4 percent higher than a year ago, not because energy got more expensive to generate but because the Commonwealth and state government rebates that had been cushioning the blow quietly expired. For households already under financial pressure, this translates to hundreds of dollars in additional annual costs. The government gave you a discount, took credit for it, then took it away. Standard procedure.

Geopolitical Desk

60,000 people crossed into Ceuta. The EU scheduled a meeting

An estimated 60,000 migrants entered Spain's North African enclave of Ceuta over three days, most by swimming. At least 88 people died. The territory's population is 84,000. The EU's response was an emergency meeting of interior ministers, where some member states used the occasion to criticise Madrid's immigration policies rather than address anything structural. Morocco's border enforcement conveniently lapsed and then resumed. The mechanism is familiar. The bodies are new.

Washington demands Iran denuclearise while offering Saudi Arabia enrichment rights

While the US presses Iran to end its nuclear programme as a condition of any Hormuz deal, a parallel US-Saudi nuclear cooperation agreement reportedly includes enrichment rights for Riyadh. The contradiction is not subtle. Granting one Gulf state the nuclear capabilities you're demanding another abandon is the kind of precedent that makes the NPT look like a suggestion rather than a treaty. The Iran-Hormuz story dominates. This one, with its longer fuse, barely registers.

Tech and Power Desk

Europe's AI Act goes live, and the first thing it regulates is honesty

The EU AI Act's transparency obligations took effect on 2 August, requiring chatbots to disclose they are not human and deepfakes to carry labels. Noncompliance can trigger fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover. The high-risk system rules, however, were quietly postponed until December 2027 — framed as an 'implementation adjustment' rather than a retreat. One might call that an optimistic relabelling, which is rather on-theme.

Audiences discover they don't trust people who are paid to be trustworthy

A growing body of data says the influencer model is fraying at the edges. The industry's response, naturally, is to rebrand the problem: 'authenticity' is now the premium product, which is a neat trick if you don't think about it too hard.

People are leaving the platform economy. The platforms have not noticed.

Facebook built a community engine that monetised the community and kept the engine. Now millions are migrating to purpose-built alternatives — Circle, Discord, Mighty Networks, even old-school forums reborn — that let people own their contact lists and run groups without an algorithm deciding who gets to see what. The new platform Chime.In launched in 2026 explicitly without algorithm-driven feeds. The strategy is brutally simple: own the contact, rent the reach.

Reclaimed Desk

The bank used to be a building. Then an app. Now it is someone else's feature.

For decades, customer inertia was one of banking's most reliable economic engines — people stayed because switching was complicated. In 2026, embedded finance, zero-click payments, and contextual services are dissolving that friction entirely. E-commerce platforms from Amazon to Mercado Libre now originate credit, insurance, and payments at point of need. The bank didn't disappear. It just stopped being the place you go.

Still Grounded

The through-line this week is institutional confidence in its own permanence. Supermarkets assume you will keep shopping. Governments assume you will keep paying. Platforms assume you will keep scrolling. Central banks assume you will keep trusting. Every one of these assumptions is still mostly correct, which is exactly why none of them feel the need to change. The gap between what institutions promise and what they deliver is not a bug. It is the product.
